A team of researchers in eastern Turkey has begun deep core drilling into the controversial Durupınar formation — a 515-foot boat-shaped geological mound that recent viral attention tied to a 440-year-old Italian world map.

The site, located in the Doğubayazıt district of Ağrı Province near Mount Ararat, has long been claimed by fringe researchers and creationists to hold the petrified remains of Noah's Ark. Mainstream geologists, however, have long dismissed the mound as a natural erosion formation.

Interest resurfaced when researchers re-examined a 1587 hand-drawn world map by Italian cartographer Urbano Monte. Monte's 60-sheet map explicitly illustrated a vessel resting high in the Ararat mountains, reigniting debate over whether ancient cartographers knew of a specific physical landmark in the valley.

Now, an expedition led by Prof. Dr. Cenker Atila of Sivas Cumhuriyet University, working alongside the research group Noah's Ark Scans, has received permits from the Turkish government to conduct subsurface testing. 

In late September, the team reported using ground-penetrating radar to map what they describe as right-angle underground anomalies and void chambers. During core drilling down to 18 metres, the project reported that a drill bit broke on an unusually dense subterranean layer, which team members claim could be petrified organic material.

Mainstream archaeologists urge caution, pointing out that rock formations often produce false radar reflections and natural mineral deposits, and that no peer-reviewed evidence has confirmed man-made timber at the site.
